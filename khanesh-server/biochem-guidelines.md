# Clinical Blood-Test Patterns and Action Thresholds

## Executive Summary

- **Confirm Before Labeling Diabetes**: Diabetes is diagnosed by A1C >=6.5%, fasting plasma glucose >=126 mg/dL, 2-hour 75-g OGTT glucose >=200 mg/dL, or random glucose >=200 mg/dL when classic hyperglycemic symptoms or crisis are present; without unequivocal hyperglycemia, obtain two abnormal results -> repeat the abnormal test and do not diagnose from one borderline or discordant result.[31]
- **Treat Biochemical Emergencies as Clinical Syndromes**: Ketones plus metabolic acidosis indicate DKA, while marked hyperglycemia with hyperosmolality and little ketosis indicates HHS -> arrange emergency assessment rather than outpatient adjustment.[78]
- **Use Risk-Based Lipid Targets**: LDL-C >=190 mg/dL warrants maximally tolerated statin therapy, while the 2026 ACC/AHA framework uses lower LDL-C goals as ASCVD risk rises, including <100 mg/dL for lower-risk severe hypercholesterolemia and <55 mg/dL for clinical ASCVD -> treat the patient and risk category, not an isolated LDL number.[12]
- **Do Not Equate Troponin With Infarction**: Troponin above the assay-specific 99th percentile establishes myocardial injury; acute MI requires a rise or fall plus clinical evidence of ischemia -> use serial high-sensitivity troponin, ECG, symptoms, and imaging in an urgent chest-pain pathway.[49]
- **Stage Kidney Disease With Two Axes**: eGFR categories G1-G5 and albuminuria categories A1-A3 jointly determine prognosis; persistent eGFR <60 or albuminuria >=30 mg/g for at least 3 months supports CKD -> confirm persistence and use the KDIGO risk grid to set monitoring, treatment, and referral intensity.[40]
- **Separate Liver Injury From Liver Function**: ALT and AST mainly signal hepatocellular injury, ALP and bilirubin signal cholestatic injury, while INR and albumin better reflect synthetic function -> calculate the R ratio, review medications and exposures, and escalate urgently when coagulopathy or encephalopathy accompanies acute injury.[1]
- **Interpret Thyroid Results as Combinations**: High TSH with low free T4 indicates primary hypothyroidism, low TSH with high free T4 or T3 indicates thyrotoxicosis, and low free T4 with low or inappropriately normal TSH suggests central disease -> order the next hormone or antibody test based on the pattern, not as a reflexive panel.[58]
- **Respect Lab-Specific Critical Limits**: Severe symptomatic hyponatremia, severe hypocalcemia, dangerous potassium abnormalities, severe acidemia, and hypoglycemia can cause neurologic or cardiac injury -> use the laboratory's critical-value policy, symptoms, ECG, and repeat confirmation when appropriate; never delay emergency care for a routine repeat.[76]
- **Hormone and Urine Tests Need Context**: Low testosterone requires symptoms plus two separate early-morning low results, primary ovarian insufficiency requires an appropriate menstrual pattern plus elevated FSH, and microhematuria is defined microscopically rather than by dipstick alone -> time sampling, exclude transient causes, and follow the disease-specific pathway.[16]

This is a clinical reference framework, not an individual diagnosis. Units, assay methods, age, pregnancy, medications, comorbidity, and local laboratory reference intervals can change interpretation. Emergency symptoms or a result reported as critical require immediate clinician or emergency-service review.

## 1. A Practical Interpretation Framework: Pattern, Probability, Action

A blood or urine result has three separate meanings: analytic abnormality, disease probability, and action urgency. A value outside a reference interval is not automatically a disease, and a value inside the interval does not exclude disease when symptoms, trends, or high-risk context are present. The most reliable approach is to identify the clinical question, check specimen quality and timing, interpret related results together, and then decide whether to repeat, investigate, treat, or refer.

The useful framework is similar to the KDIGO CGA model: classify a kidney result by cause, GFR, and albuminuria rather than creatinine alone. The same logic generalizes to other panels: diabetes uses glycemia plus symptoms and ketones; thyroid disease uses TSH plus free hormones; liver disease uses injury pattern plus synthetic function; and cardiovascular disease uses biomarkers plus symptoms, ECG, and imaging. This combination-based approach is more informative than a list of isolated flags.[40]

| Clinical question | First-line results | Pattern that changes urgency | Initial action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hyperglycemia | A1C, fasting glucose, random glucose, OGTT when indicated | Hyperglycemia plus ketones, acidosis, dehydration, altered mental status | Emergency assessment for suspected DKA or HHS; otherwise confirm and risk-stratify |
| Cardiovascular injury | ECG plus serial hs-troponin | Ischemic symptoms or ECG changes plus troponin rise or fall | Emergency ACS pathway |
| Heart failure | BNP or NT-proBNP plus examination and imaging | Dyspnea, congestion, hypoxia, or markedly elevated peptide | Urgent evaluation; peptide alone does not prove HF |
| Kidney disease | Creatinine/eGFR, uACR, urinalysis, electrolytes | eGFR <30, A3 albuminuria, active sediment, rapid decline, refractory potassium or acid-base abnormality | Nephrology or urgent review depending on severity |
| Liver injury | ALT, AST, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin | Acute injury plus INR elevation, encephalopathy, bleeding, or rapidly rising bilirubin | Same-day or emergency liver evaluation |
| Thyroid dysfunction | TSH, then free T4 and/or free T3 based on TSH | Central pattern, severe thyrotoxicosis, pregnancy, arrhythmia, or severe symptoms | Prompt endocrine or acute medical review |

The decision rule is observation -> mechanism -> implication -> action. For example, a high ALT with relatively normal ALP is an hepatocellular pattern; the mechanism may be viral, toxic, ischemic, or metabolic; the implication is that the differential differs from biliary obstruction; the action is repeat and etiologic testing rather than treating the number as a diagnosis.[1]

## 2. Diabetes, Prediabetes, and Hyperglycemic Crises

### Diagnostic thresholds and confirmation

| Test | Normal or non-diabetes range | Prediabetes | Diabetes |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A1C | <5.7% | 5.7-6.4% | >=6.5% |
| Fasting plasma glucose | <100 mg/dL | 100-125 mg/dL | >=126 mg/dL |
| 2-hour 75-g OGTT | <140 mg/dL | 140-199 mg/dL | >=200 mg/dL |
| Random plasma glucose | Not diagnostic by itself | Not a standard prediabetes test | >=200 mg/dL with classic symptoms or hyperglycemic crisis |

ADA expresses the corresponding diabetes cutoffs as A1C >=48 mmol/mol, fasting glucose >=7.0 mmol/L, and 2-hour or random glucose >=11.1 mmol/L. Fasting means no caloric intake for at least 8 hours. In an asymptomatic person without unequivocal hyperglycemia, confirm with two abnormal results from the same test on different occasions or two different tests; when tests disagree, repeat the test above its diagnostic cut point. NIDDK also cautions that a mismatch between A1C and glucose can indicate that A1C is unreliable for that person, so evaluate anemia, hemoglobin variants, altered red-cell survival, and other causes before using A1C alone.[31] [32]

### Combined patterns and action

DKA is a biochemical syndrome of hyperglycemia or known diabetes, ketonemia or ketonuria, and metabolic acidosis. The 2024 consensus criteria use glucose >=200 mg/dL or known diabetes, beta-hydroxybutyrate >=3.0 mmol/L or urine ketones at least 2+, and pH <7.30 and/or bicarbonate <18 mmol/L. HHS is characterized by glucose >=600 mg/dL, hyperosmolality, and little or no significant ketoacidosis. The newer consensus emphasizes direct beta-hydroxybutyrate measurement, serial electrolytes, renal function, venous pH, osmolality when HHS is suspected, and treatment of precipitating illness.[78]

The practical action is urgent assessment for vomiting, dehydration, abdominal pain, deep or rapid breathing, confusion, severe weakness, or elevated ketones, even if glucose is not extremely high. Euglycemic DKA can occur, particularly with SGLT2 inhibitors, so ketones and acid-base results can outweigh the glucose number. This is a clinical inference from the consensus syndrome definition and should be handled using local emergency protocols rather than home treatment.

### Guideline case study: ADA discordant testing

The ADA gives a concrete pattern: an A1C of **7.0%** followed by **6.8%** confirms diabetes because the same diagnostic test remains above the threshold on repeat testing. In contrast, if A1C and plasma glucose disagree, the test above the cut point should be repeated and the diagnosis should follow the confirmatory result. The case reveals why confirmation is a diagnostic safeguard rather than an administrative formality.[31]

## 3. Lipid Profiles and Cardiovascular Risk

### Lipid patterns and treatment thresholds

A lipid panel normally includes total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides. Non-HDL-C is useful when triglycerides are elevated because it captures cholesterol in atherogenic particles. ApoB and Lp(a) can refine risk in selected patients, but they do not replace the clinical risk calculation. HDL-C is a risk marker, not a reason to use medication solely to raise HDL-C.[12]

| Pattern or risk group | Threshold or goal from the current framework | Clinical action |
|---|---:|---|
| Severe hypercholesterolemia | LDL-C >=190 mg/dL | Start maximally tolerated statin therapy; assess secondary causes and possible familial hypercholesterolemia |
| Severe hypercholesterolemia without clinical ASCVD, HeFH, or subclinical atherosclerosis | LDL-C goal <100 mg/dL; non-HDL-C <130 mg/dL | Intensify lifestyle and lipid-lowering therapy as indicated |
| HeFH or additional risk factors, including coronary calcification in the guideline framework | LDL-C goal <70 mg/dL; non-HDL-C <100 mg/dL | Escalate therapy and consider additional agents when statin response is inadequate |
| Clinical ASCVD | LDL-C goal <55 mg/dL; non-HDL-C <85 mg/dL | Use secondary-prevention treatment and add nonstatin therapy when needed |
| Primary prevention, borderline risk | 10-year PREVENT risk 3% to <5% | Consider lipid-lowering therapy after clinician-patient discussion |
| Primary prevention, intermediate risk | 10-year PREVENT risk 5% to <10% | Consider lipid-lowering therapy after shared decision-making and risk-enhancer review |

The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline uses PREVENT equations for adults aged 30-79 in primary prevention and gives lower LDL-C goals as risk increases. Older ACC/AHA summaries remain useful for the principle that LDL-C >=190 mg/dL is treated without relying on a 10-year risk estimate, but current targets and risk equations should follow the current guideline and local practice.[12] [13]

### Triglycerides and risk enhancers

Marked triglyceride elevation should prompt a search for diabetes, alcohol exposure, hypothyroidism, kidney disease, obesity, medications, and genetic disorders. The higher the triglyceride result, the more important it becomes to distinguish ASCVD risk management from pancreatitis prevention; do not use the LDL-only pathway when triglycerides are very high. A fasting repeat may be appropriate when the sample is nonfasting or unexpectedly abnormal, but severe symptoms or very high values require prompt clinical review.[13]

Risk-enhancing results include elevated Lp(a), apoB, hsCRP, persistent triglyceride elevation, chronic kidney disease, diabetes-specific factors, inflammatory disease, and premature family history. These results modify a risk discussion; they are not standalone diagnoses. Coronary artery calcium can help resolve uncertainty in selected primary-prevention adults, but a zero score does not erase very high LDL-C, diabetes, smoking, or strong family-history risk.[12]

### Case study: risk category changes the decision

The 2026 guideline contrasts two decisions. An LDL-C result of 190 mg/dL triggers maximally tolerated statin therapy even before a calculated risk score, whereas an LDL-C in the primary-prevention range is interpreted through PREVENT risk, risk enhancers, and shared decision-making. The mechanism is treatment benefit proportional to baseline risk and lifetime exposure, so the action is not determined by the LDL number alone.[12]

## 4. Cardiovascular Biomarkers: Troponin and Natriuretic Peptides

### Troponin pattern

High-sensitivity cardiac troponin is the preferred biomarker for myocardial injury in suspected acute coronary syndrome. The decision limit is the assay-specific 99th percentile upper reference limit; there is no single universal troponin number because assays differ. A value above that limit means myocardial injury, not automatically myocardial infarction.[49] [22]

Acute MI requires a rise and/or fall in troponin with at least one value above the 99th percentile plus clinical evidence of ischemia, such as ischemic symptoms, new ischemic ECG changes, imaging evidence of new loss of viable myocardium or new regional wall-motion abnormality, or a coronary thrombus. Chronic kidney disease, tachyarrhythmia, heart failure, myocarditis, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, and strenuous exercise can elevate troponin without type 1 MI. The implication is that an isolated stable elevation needs context, while a changing result with ischemic symptoms needs urgent care.[50]

### BNP and NT-proBNP pattern

For nonacute evaluation, BNP above **35 pg/mL** or NT-proBNP at least **125 pg/mL** supports consideration of heart failure, while values below those thresholds make HF less likely in the referenced primary-care framework. In acute dyspnea, rule-out cutoffs and age-adjusted rule-in values differ, and renal dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, age, obesity, and acute illness change peptide concentrations. Use the local acute-care algorithm rather than transferring outpatient thresholds to the emergency department.[23]

Natriuretic peptides answer a probability question, not an anatomic one. An elevated result should lead to examination, ECG, chest imaging or echocardiography as appropriate; a low result can be falsely reassuring in obesity or very early disease. Clinical congestion, hypoxia, hypotension, and new arrhythmia determine urgency more than the peptide alone.

### Case study: injury versus infarction

The Universal Definition and AHA/ACC approach deliberately separate myocardial injury from MI. The decision is to use the 99th percentile and serial change to identify injury, then require ischemic evidence before labeling infarction. This reduces the risk of treating every troponin elevation in CKD or sepsis as an acute coronary occlusion, while still escalating a dynamic troponin pattern with chest pain.[22]

## 5. Kidney Chemistry and Urinalysis

### eGFR and albuminuria staging

CKD requires an abnormality of kidney structure or function present for at least **3 months**. Either eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a marker such as albuminuria can meet the disease definition when chronicity is established. G1 or G2 alone does not establish CKD without another marker of kidney damage.[40] [37]

| eGFR category | eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m2 | Albuminuria category | uACR, mg/g |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| G1 | >=90 | A1, normal to mildly increased | <30 |
| G2 | 60-89 | A2, moderately increased | 30-300 |
| G3a | 45-59 | A3, severely increased | >300 |
| G3b | 30-44 |  |  |
| G4 | 15-29 |  |  |
| G5 | <15 |  |  |

The table is a two-axis classification, not a list of interchangeable stages. A patient with preserved eGFR and A3 albuminuria can have substantial risk, while an older person with G2 and no marker of damage may not have CKD. Confirm an unexpected uACR with a properly collected sample and repeat testing because exercise, fever, infection, menstruation, heart failure, and marked hyperglycemia can transiently increase albumin excretion.[40]

### Action thresholds

Persistent A2 or A3 albuminuria, eGFR <60, a sustained fall in eGFR, resistant hypertension, active urine sediment, unexplained electrolyte or acid-base disturbance, or eGFR <30 warrants intensified kidney evaluation. KDIGO recommends using the combined GFR-albuminuria risk grid and kidney-failure risk to guide monitoring and referral; G4, G5, A3, rapid decline, and suspected glomerulonephritis generally need nephrology involvement. Diabetes with albuminuria or reduced eGFR should trigger evidence-based kidney and cardiovascular risk management, including consideration of renin-angiotensin system blockade for appropriate hypertension with albuminuria and SGLT2-inhibitor therapy when eligibility criteria are met.[40]

### Urinalysis combinations

| Urine pattern | Likely mechanism or differential | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrite positive plus leukocyte esterase or pyuria | Bacteriuria with urinary inflammation, often UTI | Correlate with symptoms; culture when indicated, especially in pregnancy, men, recurrent infection, systemic illness, or complicated disease |
| Dipstick blood positive but few or no RBCs | Hemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria, or false positive | Microscopy and clinical review; consider CK if muscle injury is possible |
| >=3 RBC/HPF on microscopy | Microhematuria | Exclude infection, stones, menstruation, and other benign causes; repeat or use risk-based urologic evaluation |
| Protein plus dysmorphic RBCs or RBC casts | Glomerular bleeding | Quantify uACR or protein/creatinine ratio and seek nephrology review |
| Glucose plus ketones with hyperglycemia and low bicarbonate | Possible DKA | Check beta-hydroxybutyrate, electrolytes, pH, and urgent treatment pathway |
| WBC casts | Renal inflammation or pyelonephritis | Clinical review, culture, renal evaluation when persistent or systemic |

AUA defines microhematuria by microscopy, not dipstick alone. In a low or negligible-risk patient, the 2025 AUA/SUFU guideline recommends repeat urinalysis within 6 months rather than immediate cystoscopy; persistent or higher-risk hematuria follows a risk-based imaging and cystoscopy pathway.[55] AAFP: Office-Based Urinalysis

### Case study: the kidney signal is not one number

KDIGO's G-A framework is a practical case study in avoiding creatinine anchoring. The decision to monitor, treat, or refer changes when the same eGFR is paired with A1 versus A3 albuminuria, because albuminuria adds independent prognostic information. The outcome is a more proportionate plan: repeat and monitor low-risk findings, but intensify evaluation for persistent albuminuria, active sediment, or low eGFR.[40]

## 6. Liver Chemistry Patterns and Urgent Signals

### Injury pattern

The AASLD approach begins by separating injury pattern from function. ALT and AST predominant elevation suggests hepatocellular injury; ALP and bilirubin predominant elevation suggests cholestasis; both may be elevated in a mixed pattern. GGT can support a hepatic source of an elevated ALP, while bilirubin should be fractionated when the cause is unclear. Albumin and INR are more relevant to hepatic synthetic function than the aminotransferases themselves.[1]

The R ratio formalizes the pattern:

`R = (ALT / ALT upper limit of normal) / (ALP / ALP upper limit of normal)`

| R ratio | Pattern | Common direction of evaluation |
|---:|---|---|
| >=5 | Hepatocellular | Viral, autoimmune, ischemic, toxic or drug-related, metabolic and other hepatic causes |
| >2 to <5 | Mixed | Evaluate both hepatocellular and cholestatic causes |
| <=2 | Cholestatic | Biliary obstruction, cholestatic drug injury, infiltrative or other cholestatic disease |

The ratio is a classification aid, not a diagnosis. Recheck unexpected results, review alcohol and all prescribed, over-the-counter, and herbal products, assess viral and metabolic risks, and use ultrasound or additional imaging when cholestasis or obstruction is possible. The magnitude, trajectory, symptoms, and bilirubin/INR matter more than a single mild flag.[1]

### Critical patterns

Acute liver failure is an emergency pattern of acute liver injury with coagulopathy, generally INR >=1.5, and hepatic encephalopathy in a person without established cirrhosis or another explanation for the encephalopathy. Jaundice, confusion, bleeding, hypoglycemia, rapidly worsening INR, or rapidly rising bilirubin requires urgent hospital assessment and early specialist or transplant-center discussion.[2]

For suspected drug-induced liver injury, the FDA describes a Hy's Law signal as ALT or AST >3 times the upper limit of normal with total bilirubin >2 times the upper limit of normal, occurring without a prominent cholestatic explanation and without another clear cause. This is a safety signal, not proof that a particular drug caused the injury; it should trigger medication review, repeat testing, exclusion of competing causes, and clinician-directed decisions about stopping or changing therapy.[87]

### Case study: injury versus function

A patient can have a striking ALT elevation with preserved INR and albumin, or modest aminotransferase elevations with a dangerous INR rise. The AASLD framework therefore separates the decision to investigate injury from the decision to escalate for loss of function. The mechanism is that enzymes leak from injured cells, whereas INR reflects impaired synthesis or severe systemic disturbance; the action must follow both dimensions, not enzyme magnitude alone.[1]

## 7. Thyroid Function Tests

### Primary, central, and subclinical patterns

| TSH | Free T4 | Free T3 | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | Low | Usually not needed initially | Overt primary hypothyroidism |
| High | Normal | Usually normal | Subclinical primary hypothyroidism |
| Low or suppressed | High | May be high | Overt thyrotoxicosis or hyperthyroidism |
| Low or suppressed | Normal | Normal | Subclinical hyperthyroidism, early disease, medication effect, or non-thyroidal illness |
| Low or normal, inappropriately so | Low | Low or normal | Central hypothyroidism; evaluate pituitary or hypothalamic disease |

The ATA describes a low TSH with elevated free T4 as a hyperthyroid pattern and a low TSH with low free T4 as a central hypothyroid pattern. In primary disease, TSH usually moves opposite to free T4. When pituitary disease, pregnancy, severe illness, or medication interference is possible, do not use TSH alone.[58]

NICE recommends measuring TSH first in adults when pituitary disease is not suspected. If TSH is above the reference range, measure free T4; if TSH is below the reference range, measure free T4 and free T3. Consider levothyroxine for adults with TSH >=10 mIU/L on two tests 3 months apart. In adults younger than 65 with symptoms and TSH above the reference range but <10 mIU/L, NICE allows a 6-month treatment trial after confirmation and clinical discussion.[28]

### Antibodies, pregnancy, and action

TPO antibodies support autoimmune thyroiditis in the appropriate high-TSH pattern. TRAb supports Graves disease when low TSH is accompanied by thyrotoxicosis. Antibodies support etiology and prognosis; they do not replace TSH and free-hormone interpretation. Marked thyrotoxicosis with fever, delirium, heart failure, or severe tachycardia is an emergency clinical syndrome, not a diagnosis made from TSH alone.[58]

Pregnancy requires trimester-specific reference intervals and closer monitoring. The ATA pregnancy guidance states that levothyroxine dosing is adjusted to target TSH between the lower reference limit and 2.5 mU/L in the referenced pregnancy-management context. A pregnant person with abnormal TSH, low free T4, positive antibodies, or significant symptoms should receive prompt obstetric or endocrine review rather than applying nonpregnant intervals.[71]

### Case study: central hypothyroidism

The central pattern is a useful failure case for reflex testing. A low free T4 with a low or normal TSH might be incorrectly called normal if TSH is treated as the sole gatekeeper; the ATA pattern instead directs pituitary evaluation. The decision is to investigate central disease and assess other pituitary axes, not to wait for TSH to become high.[58]

## 8. Electrolytes, Acid-Base Results, and Vitamins

### Electrolyte action table

Critical values are laboratory-specific, and symptoms or ECG findings can make a less extreme result dangerous. The following thresholds are practical red flags, not substitutes for the reporting laboratory's critical-value policy.

| Result pattern | Clinical concern | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium <120 mEq/L with seizure, severe confusion, or coma | Acute symptomatic severe hyponatremia and cerebral edema risk | Emergency assessment; hypertonic 3% saline is used in specialist protocols, with closely controlled correction |
| Sodium <125 mEq/L, rapid fall, or significant neurologic symptoms | Severe or rapidly evolving hyponatremia | Urgent review, assess volume status, osmolality, glucose, medications, and cause |
| Potassium reported as critical, rapidly rising, or accompanied by weakness or ECG change | Arrhythmia risk or pseudohyperkalemia | Immediate ECG and urgent clinician assessment; repeat a non-hemolyzed sample only when clinically safe |
| Potassium markedly low or with weakness, arrhythmia, or ECG change | Ventricular arrhythmia and muscle weakness risk | Urgent ECG, magnesium assessment, and monitored replacement |
| Total calcium <7.5 mg/dL, approximately 1.9 mmol/L, or symptomatic tetany/QT prolongation | Severe hypocalcemia | Urgent assessment, check ionized calcium, magnesium, albumin, renal function, and cause; intravenous calcium may be required |
| Bicarbonate <18 mmol/L with hyperglycemia and ketones | DKA pattern | Emergency hyperglycemic-crisis pathway; assess pH, beta-hydroxybutyrate, potassium, and renal function |
| Low magnesium with arrhythmia, seizures, or refractory hypokalemia/hypocalcemia | Neuromuscular and cardiac instability | Urgent replacement and search for gastrointestinal, renal, medication, or endocrine cause |

The source for symptomatic severe hyponatremia describes 3% saline for serum sodium <120 mEq/L with seizures, severe confusion, or coma. Merck's hypocalcemia guidance supports urgent treatment when hypocalcemia is severe or symptomatic. These are emergency patterns; the rate and method of correction must follow monitored protocols because overcorrection of chronic hyponatremia can injure the brain.[76] [74]

### Vitamin results

| Test | Useful interpretation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 25-hydroxyvitamin D | <12 ng/mL is deficient; 12 to <20 ng/mL is generally inadequate; >=20 ng/mL is adequate for most people; >50 ng/mL may be associated with adverse effects | Treat deficiency in context, avoid megadosing, and consider calcium, renal, parathyroid, malabsorption, and medication context |
| Serum vitamin B12 | ODS notes that experts suggest methylmalonic acid when serum B12 is 150-399 pg/mL because a borderline serum result may not settle tissue deficiency | Correlate with CBC, neurologic findings, diet, medications, renal function, and MMA; do not exclude deficiency from a borderline value |
| Serum folate | Interpret with CBC/MCV, diet, pregnancy, medications, and B12 status; recent intake can influence serum values | Correct confirmed deficiency but assess B12 before or alongside folate when macrocytosis or neurologic symptoms are present |

NIH ODS presents vitamin D cut points as population guidance rather than a universal treatment target for every patient. The Endocrine Society has also cautioned against routine vitamin D screening or rigid serum targets in generally healthy people without an indication. Vitamin B12 interpretation is especially dependent on assay and context; a borderline value is a reason for confirmatory metabolic testing, not an automatic diagnosis.[97] [7] [63] [6]

## 9. Sex Hormones and Reproductive Endocrinology

### Male androgen pattern

The AUA uses total testosterone below **300 ng/dL** as a reasonable cutoff supporting low testosterone, but diagnosis requires compatible symptoms or signs and consistently low measurements. The Endocrine Society recommends an accurate morning fasting total testosterone measurement and confirmation by repeating a morning fasting value. Acute illness, poor sleep, obesity, medications, exogenous androgens, opioids, glucocorticoids, and assay variation can lower results.[16] [17]

After confirmed low testosterone, LH distinguishes a likely primary testicular pattern from a central pattern. High LH supports primary testicular failure; low or inappropriately normal LH supports hypothalamic or pituitary suppression. The AUA pathway recommends prolactin testing when testosterone is low with low or low-normal LH, and pituitary evaluation when the result or symptoms suggest central disease. Do not start testosterone solely from a single afternoon result or without considering fertility, hematocrit, prostate risk, sleep apnea, and contraindications.[16]

### Female sex hormones

FSH, LH, estradiol, progesterone, testosterone, SHBG, and DHEAS are strongly dependent on cycle day, age, pregnancy status, contraception, and assay. A single estradiol or progesterone value therefore has limited meaning without timing. Menopause is often a clinical diagnosis in the appropriate age and symptom context, while premature or primary ovarian insufficiency requires a disease-specific assessment rather than an isolated low estradiol.

The 2024 evidence-based POI guideline uses a compatible clinical pattern of disordered or absent ovarian function with elevated FSH; the guideline identifies FSH >25 IU/L as the diagnostic biochemical threshold in the appropriate setting and recommends repeat testing when the diagnosis remains uncertain. Pregnancy and other causes of amenorrhea must be considered. This is not interchangeable with interpreting FSH in a person taking hormonal contraception or in a routine cycle.[92]

Biochemical hyperandrogenism should be interpreted with clinical signs, reliable assays, and exclusion of mimics such as thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia, pregnancy, and androgen exposure. Markedly high androgens, virilization, or rapidly progressive symptoms require prompt evaluation for an ovarian or adrenal source; no single universal testosterone cutoff can safely distinguish every cause across assays.

### Case study: timing is part of the test

The testosterone guidelines make timing a diagnostic decision: the same person may have a different result in the morning, afternoon, during acute illness, or after poor sleep. The decision to repeat a low morning value before treatment protects against labeling transient suppression as hypogonadism, while the LH and prolactin pattern determines whether the next step is testicular evaluation or pituitary investigation.[16]

## 10. Integrated Clinical Action Matrix

| Finding combination | Most useful interpretation | Recommended next step |
|---|---|---|
| A1C 5.7-6.4% or fasting glucose 100-125 mg/dL | Prediabetes range, not diabetes | Lifestyle and cardiovascular-risk assessment; repeat or perform an appropriate confirmatory/risk test |
| A1C >=6.5% plus repeat abnormal result | Diabetes confirmed in the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia | Classify type, assess complications and cardiovascular/kidney risk, and start guideline-based care |
| Glucose >=200 mg/dL plus ketones plus bicarbonate <18 mmol/L or pH <7.30 | DKA pattern | Emergency treatment and precipitant evaluation |
| LDL-C >=190 mg/dL | Severe hypercholesterolemia | Maximally tolerated statin, secondary-cause review, and familial hypercholesterolemia assessment |
| Chest pain plus dynamic hs-troponin above the assay 99th percentile | Possible acute MI | Immediate ECG, serial biomarkers, and emergency ACS evaluation |
| Dyspnea plus elevated BNP or NT-proBNP | Possible HF | Examine for congestion and obtain ECG/imaging; peptide does not establish etiology |
| eGFR <60 or uACR >=30 mg/g persisting at least 3 months | CKD pattern | Stage by G and A categories, review blood pressure and diabetes, and set monitoring/referral plan |
| Proteinuria plus RBC casts or dysmorphic RBCs | Glomerular disease pattern | Quantify protein, check renal function and serologies as indicated, and refer to nephrology |
| ALT/AST predominant elevation with R >=5 | Hepatocellular injury pattern | Medication, viral, autoimmune, metabolic, ischemic, and imaging evaluation as clinically indicated |
| ALP/bilirubin predominant elevation with R <=2 | Cholestatic injury pattern | Confirm hepatic ALP source and assess biliary obstruction or infiltrative disease |
| High TSH plus low free T4 | Overt primary hypothyroidism | Treat or refer according to severity, pregnancy, age, symptoms, and comorbidity |
| Low TSH plus high free T4 or T3 | Overt thyrotoxicosis | Determine cause, assess arrhythmia and systemic severity, and treat promptly |
| Low free T4 plus low or normal TSH | Possible central hypothyroidism | Pituitary and other-axis assessment; do not rely on TSH alone |
| Microhematuria >=3 RBC/HPF after transient causes excluded | Persistent urinary blood | Repeat in low-risk patients or follow AUA risk-based evaluation |
| Borderline B12 150-399 pg/mL plus macrocytosis or neurologic symptoms | Possible tissue B12 deficiency | Check MMA and treat based on clinical assessment rather than serum B12 alone |

The matrix is intentionally conservative. It identifies when a result combination changes probability and action, but it does not replace examination, medication review, repeat sampling, imaging, or specialist judgment. Critical results with symptoms override the normal outpatient confirmation sequence.

## Synthesis

The major panels differ in what makes a result diagnostic. Diabetes has explicit cut points, but confirmation is required unless hyperglycemia is unequivocal. Lipids are risk-stratification measurements, so treatment targets change with primary prevention, severe hypercholesterolemia, HeFH, coronary calcium, and established ASCVD. Troponin is a biomarker of injury whose meaning depends on serial change and ischemia, while BNP and NT-proBNP are probability tools affected by renal function, age, rhythm, and body habitus.

Kidney testing combines an organ-function axis, eGFR, with a damage axis, albuminuria, and adds chronicity. Liver testing contrasts an injury axis, ALT, AST, ALP, and bilirubin, with a function axis, INR and albumin. Thyroid testing is a feedback-loop problem in which the same TSH value means different things depending on free T4, pituitary integrity, pregnancy, illness, and medication interference. Electrolytes are less forgiving: symptoms, ECG, rate of change, and the reporting laboratory's critical limit can outweigh a population reference interval.

| Dimension | Diabetes | Lipids | Troponin/BNP | Kidney | Liver | Thyroid and hormones |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main mechanism | Glycemic exposure and acute metabolic decompensation | Atherogenic particle burden and baseline risk | Myocardial injury or wall stress | Filtration plus albumin leakage | Cellular injury plus synthetic capacity | Feedback-loop or gonadal-axis dysfunction |
| Most important combination | Glucose/A1C plus symptoms, ketones, pH, bicarbonate | LDL-C plus ASCVD risk and enhancers | Biomarker plus serial change, ECG, symptoms, imaging | eGFR plus uACR plus chronicity | ALT/AST versus ALP/bilirubin plus INR | TSH plus free hormone plus timing/physiology |
| Typical uncertainty | A1C-glucose discordance and altered red-cell biology | Risk estimates and assay variation | Chronic elevation versus acute injury | Acute kidney injury versus chronic disease | Mild nonspecific enzyme elevation | Central disease, pregnancy, drugs, cycle timing |
| Highest-risk failure | Missed DKA or HHS | Undertreated LDL >=190 or secondary prevention | Calling every troponin elevation MI or missing dynamic MI | Missing A3 albuminuria or active sediment | Missing acute liver failure | Calling central disease normal or missing severe thyrotoxicosis |
| Action horizon | Confirm, then manage chronic risk; emergency for crisis | Long-term prevention, urgent review for very high values or symptoms | Minutes to hours when ischemia is possible | Repeat and stage, urgent referral for severe or active disease | Same-day or emergency when INR, encephalopathy, or bleeding is present | Routine, prompt, or emergency depending on pattern and physiology |

The central tension is between numeric thresholds and clinical context. Thresholds make decisions reproducible, but they are not interchangeable across assays or populations. A strong workflow therefore uses the threshold to identify the next question, the combination of tests to refine probability, and the symptoms and trajectory to determine urgency. The recommendation is to document the result, unit, reference interval, specimen timing, relevant medications, comparison with prior values, and an explicit next action: repeat, confirm, investigate, treat, refer, or send to emergency care.
