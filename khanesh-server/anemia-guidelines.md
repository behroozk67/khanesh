# CBC-Based Classification of Anemia by Diagnostic Pattern

## Executive Summary

- **WHO First Gate**: WHO's 2024 guideline defines anemia by hemoglobin, not by MCV. The retrieved adult cutoffs are **<120 g/L** for nonpregnant women aged 15-65 years, **<130 g/L** for men aged 15-65 years, and **<110 g/L** for pregnancy -> apply the age, sex, and pregnancy-specific hemoglobin cutoff before classifying morphology. [16]
- **Morphology Plus Mechanism**: MCV separates microcytic, normocytic, and macrocytic patterns, but the reticulocyte response distinguishes impaired production from blood loss or red-cell destruction -> interpret every MCV branch with an absolute reticulocyte count or reticulocyte production index. [34]
- **Ferritin Requires Context**: WHO states that ferritin rises with inflammation. In an individual with infection or inflammation, iron deficiency may be indicated by ferritin **<30 ug/L in children** or **<70 ug/L in adults**, with CRP and AGP used in population settings -> do not exclude iron deficiency from a normal or high ferritin alone. [62]
- **Microcytosis Is a Differential, Not a Diagnosis**: Iron deficiency and thalassemia trait are common causes, but anemia of inflammation, lead toxicity, and sideroblastic anemia also produce microcytosis -> use ferritin first, then transferrin saturation, TIBC or transferrin, serum iron, hemoglobin analysis, smear, and selected marrow or genetic testing. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed
- **Hemolysis Has a Reproducible Signature**: BSH describes autoimmune hemolysis with increased reticulocytes, increased unconjugated bilirubin, reduced haptoglobin, variable or increased LDH, characteristic smear findings, and usually a positive DAT -> confirm destruction biochemically rather than relying on MCV or RDW. [2]
- **Macrocytosis Needs Two Branches**: B12 and folate deficiency cause megaloblastic macrocytosis, whereas alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, drugs, reticulocytosis, and MDS are nonmegaloblastic or marrow-related possibilities -> pair the CBC with a blood film, B12 and folate testing, liver and thyroid assessment, medication review, and marrow evaluation when indicated. [21]
- **Marrow Failure Looks Different From Hemolysis**: BSH and ASH describe acquired aplastic anemia as pancytopenia with hypocellular marrow and exclusion of mimics, whereas hemolytic anemia is usually reticulocyte-responsive -> a low reticulocyte response with additional cytopenias should trigger a marrow-failure pathway. [48]; ASH 2026 aplastic anemia guideline
- **No Single Universal Matrix Exists**: WHO primarily supplies hemoglobin and ferritin guidance, BSH supplies disease-specific guidance, and ASH supplies disease-specific and educational material. The matrix below is therefore a clinically useful synthesis of typical patterns, not a verbatim table from one ASH, BSH, or WHO document -> treat every row as suggestive, not pathognomonic.

## WHO Hemoglobin Thresholds and the First CBC Gate

WHO's 2024 guideline is the correct starting point for deciding whether a hemoglobin value meets the definition of anemia. MCV, MCH, RDW, reticulocytes, and iron studies describe the phenotype and mechanism after anemia has been established; they do not replace the hemoglobin definition. The guideline uses population-specific cutoffs and a statistical approach based on the fifth percentile of healthy reference populations. [16]

| Population in the retrieved WHO table | Hemoglobin cutoff defining anemia | Practical implication |
|---|---:|---|
| Adults 15-65 years, nonpregnant women | <120 g/L | Do not use the male cutoff or a pregnancy cutoff. |
| Adults 15-65 years, men | <130 g/L | Confirm the laboratory unit and population group. |
| Pregnancy | <110 g/L in the retrieved pregnancy row | Use the WHO pregnancy table and local obstetric context rather than a nonpregnant adult cutoff. |

The WHO 2024 individual-level severity text retrieved through the WHO-hosted chapter describes mild anemia as hemoglobin above **100 g/L** but below the applicable anemia cutoff, moderate anemia as **70-100 g/L**, and severe anemia as **<70 g/L**. These severity categories should not be confused with older tables that used different limits. [20]

WHO also specifies smoking adjustments in the retrieved guideline. The adjustment is **3 g/L** when smoking quantity is unknown or is below 10 cigarettes per day, **5 g/L** for 10-19 cigarettes per day, and **6 g/L** for more than 20 cigarettes per day. The adjustment can be added to the cutoff or subtracted from the observed hemoglobin, according to the guideline's implementation approach. [16]

**Decision-ready insight:** apply the correct WHO hemoglobin gate first, then document the MCV and reticulocyte phenotype. A CBC with an apparently modest hemoglobin reduction can be classified differently in a man, a nonpregnant woman, a pregnant patient, or a child.

## A Practical CBC-to-Mechanism Framework

The most efficient diagnostic framework is a sequence rather than a single test. Start with the CBC, red-cell indices, platelet and white-cell counts, and peripheral smear. Then use the reticulocyte response to decide whether the marrow is responding, and use iron studies to decide whether iron supply is absent, sequestered, or excessive. Merck identifies the reticulocyte count as a crucial test because it assesses the marrow response to anemia and helps distinguish production failure from blood loss or destruction. [34]

| Variable | Pattern to record | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Hemoglobin and hematocrit | Low relative to the applicable WHO and laboratory reference | Establishes anemia and severity; do not infer cause from Hb alone. |
| MCV | Microcytic, commonly <80 fL in adults; normocytic; or macrocytic, commonly above the adult reference range | Organizes the differential but cannot distinguish all causes. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed |
| MCH | Low, normal, or high relative to the laboratory range | Low MCH supports hypochromia and restricted hemoglobin synthesis; a high MCH often reflects larger cells. MCH is not a substitute for MCHC or iron studies. |
| RDW | Normal, mildly increased, or markedly increased | Measures size heterogeneity. High RDW supports evolving deficiency, reticulocytosis, mixed deficiency, or transfused mixed populations, but it is not disease-specific. |
| Reticulocytes | Absolute count, reticulocyte percentage, and preferably corrected reticulocyte production index | High or appropriately increased suggests blood loss or destruction; low or inappropriately normal suggests underproduction. The production index is more useful than an uncorrected percentage when Hb is low. [118] |
| Ferritin | Low, normal, or high, interpreted with inflammation | Reflects iron stores but is also an acute-phase reactant. WHO recommends context-sensitive interpretation. [62] |
| Serum iron, transferrin or TIBC, and TSAT | Combine rather than use serum iron alone | Low serum iron occurs in both absolute deficiency and inflammation. TIBC or transferrin and TSAT help distinguish absent iron stores from iron sequestration. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed |

MCH generally moves in the same direction as MCV because both are influenced by cell size, but it adds a hypochromia signal. RDW is especially valuable when the mean MCV looks normal: two red-cell populations can average to a normal MCV while retaining substantial size variation. This is an interpretive rule, not a disease-specific cutoff, and it is why the smear remains important.

**Decision-ready insight:** the combination of MCV and reticulocyte response is more informative than either result alone. A low reticulocyte response with low MCV points toward restricted hemoglobin production; a high reticulocyte response with normal or high MCV points toward blood loss or hemolysis.

## Microcytic and Hypochromic Anemias

Microcytosis is commonly defined as an adult MCV below 80 fL. The major diagnostic fork is between absolute iron deficiency, iron-restricted erythropoiesis from inflammation, globin-production disorders, and impaired heme synthesis. The PubMed review on microcytosis identifies iron deficiency and thalassemia trait as common causes and also lists anemia of chronic disease, lead toxicity, and sideroblastic anemia. It recommends ferritin as the first laboratory test and, when ferritin is not low, further evaluation with TIBC, TSAT, serum iron, and possibly hemoglobin electrophoresis. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed

| Cause | MCV | MCH | RDW | Reticulocytes | Iron studies and confirmatory clues |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absolute iron deficiency anemia, including chronic blood loss | Low, although early deficiency can be normocytic | Low | Usually high, especially as deficiency evolves | Low or inappropriately normal before treatment; may rise after effective iron replacement | Ferritin low and TSAT low are the key pattern. Serum iron is low. Transferrin or TIBC is often increased in absolute deficiency. If inflammation is present, use WHO's adjusted ferritin interpretation and the full iron panel. [62]; Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed |
| Anemia of inflammation or chronic disease | Normal or mildly low | Normal or mildly low | Normal or mildly high | Low or inappropriately normal | Serum iron low, TIBC or transferrin low or not increased, TSAT low, ferritin normal or high because iron is sequestered and ferritin rises with inflammation. A low ferritin can still reveal coexisting absolute deficiency. [94]; Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed; [62] |
| Beta-thalassemia trait or other thalassemia trait | Often disproportionately low relative to the degree of anemia | Low | Often normal or only mildly increased compared with iron deficiency | Normal or mildly increased; do not use reticulocytes alone to distinguish trait from deficiency | Ferritin and TSAT are usually not depleted unless iron deficiency coexists. Beta-thalassemia trait is supported by increased HbA2 on hemoglobin analysis. Normal or increased ferritin with microcytosis favors thalassemia over uncomplicated iron deficiency. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed; [11] |
| Clinically significant thalassemia, including intermedia or transfusion-dependent disease | Markedly low in beta-thalassemia syndromes; may be modified by transfusion or coexisting disease | Low | Often increased | Variable and often increased when hemolysis is prominent, but ineffective erythropoiesis can make the response inappropriate for the degree of anemia | Iron may be normal initially and high with transfusion or iron loading. Hemoglobin analysis, family history, smear, and molecular testing establish the subtype; iron studies are needed to detect coexisting deficiency or overload. [26] |
| Sideroblastic anemia, congenital or acquired | Congenital forms are usually microcytic; acquired forms may be macrocytic | Low or variable | Often high | Low or inappropriately normal because erythropoiesis is ineffective | Serum iron, ferritin, and TSAT are usually increased or show iron loading rather than depletion. Blood film and marrow examination for ring sideroblasts, plus medication, toxin, alcohol, and MDS assessment, are required. [83]; [81] |
| Lead toxicity | Often low, but can be variable | Low or normal | Variable or high | Low or inappropriately normal | Iron indices are not diagnostic and can be normal or increased. Basophilic stippling, exposure history, and blood lead measurement are more useful. Lead toxicity is part of the established microcytosis differential. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed |
| Rare inherited iron-transport or heme-synthesis defects | Usually microcytic, but the pattern depends on the defect | Usually low | Variable | Variable and often inadequate | Ferritin and serum iron can be low, normal, or high depending on the defect. Specialist hemoglobin, enzyme, genetic, and sometimes marrow testing is needed; do not force these disorders into the iron-deficiency pattern. [12] |

The most important contradiction in this group is that both iron deficiency and inflammation can produce low serum iron. The mechanism differs: absolute deficiency means stores are depleted, whereas inflammation restricts access to stored iron and can raise ferritin. The action is to read ferritin, TSAT, TIBC or transferrin, and inflammatory markers together rather than treating serum iron as a stand-alone diagnosis.

### Illustrative case study: iron deficiency versus beta-thalassemia trait

An adult with microcytosis and low MCH should not automatically receive an iron-deficiency label. The first decision is ferritin; if ferritin is low, the pattern supports iron deficiency and the next clinical decision is to identify the source of iron loss. If ferritin is not low, the next decision is to review TSAT, TIBC or transferrin, serum iron, the RBC count and smear, and hemoglobin analysis. This sequence follows the microcytosis evaluation described in the retrieved literature. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed

If beta-thalassemia trait is the explanation, HbA2 elevation supports the diagnosis and iron therapy should not be used as a substitute for hemoglobin analysis. If both disorders coexist, iron deficiency can distort the usual thalassemia pattern; a normal MCV cannot be used to exclude a mixed disorder. The practical outcome is a targeted test pathway rather than repeated empiric iron treatment.

**Decision-ready insight:** low MCV plus low ferritin is a strong iron-deficiency pattern, but low MCV plus normal or high ferritin is a request for hemoglobin analysis and inflammation-aware iron interpretation, not a reason to stop investigating.

## Normocytic Anemias

Normocytic anemia is a broad phenotype that includes underproduction, acute blood loss, hemolysis, kidney disease, inflammation, endocrine disease, marrow failure, and early nutritional deficiency. The reticulocyte response is the main discriminator. A high or appropriately increased response supports loss or destruction; a low or inappropriately normal response supports insufficient production. [34]

| Cause | MCV | MCH | RDW | Reticulocytes | Iron studies and confirmatory clues |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acute blood loss | Initially normocytic and normochromic | Usually normal | Initially normal; may increase as reticulocytes enter blood or iron deficiency develops | Initially normal or low, then increases after marrow response | Iron studies can be initially unrevealing; chronic or ongoing blood loss produces absolute iron deficiency. Search for the bleeding source when iron deficiency is established. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed |
| Immune or nonimmune hemolytic anemia | Usually normocytic; can become macrocytic from reticulocytosis and polychromasia | Usually normal | Often high | Increased and appropriate unless the marrow cannot respond | Iron studies are not the primary discriminator. Confirm hemolysis with unconjugated bilirubin, LDH, haptoglobin, smear, and DAT when immune hemolysis is suspected. [2]; [90] |
| Anemia of inflammation, early or predominantly normocytic | Normal | Normal or mildly low | Normal or mildly high | Low or inappropriately normal | Serum iron and TSAT low, TIBC or transferrin low or not increased, ferritin normal or high. Inflammation can conceal coexisting absolute iron deficiency. [94]; [62] |
| Chronic kidney disease or other erythropoietin-deficient states | Usually normal | Usually normal | Normal or increased if comorbidity is present | Low or inappropriately normal | Ferritin and TSAT must be interpreted with the kidney disease context; functional iron restriction can coexist with stored iron. Kidney-specific treatment thresholds are not interchangeable with WHO population ferritin cutoffs. [98] |
| Aplastic anemia or severe marrow failure | Normocytic or mildly macrocytic | Normal or mildly high | Variable | Low or markedly inadequate for the degree of anemia | Iron studies do not establish the diagnosis. The defining pattern is pancytopenia with hypocellular marrow, after excluding mimics such as hypoplastic MDS; BSH refers to Camitta criteria for severity. [48]; ASH 2026 aplastic anemia guideline |
| Pure red-cell or other selective marrow failure | Usually normal or macrocytic | Normal or high | Variable | Markedly low or absent relative to the anemia | Iron studies are usually not diagnostic. The key distinction is an isolated red-cell production failure versus pancytopenic aplasia; marrow examination and exclusion of secondary causes are required. |
| Marrow infiltration, acute leukemia, or advanced MDS with a normocytic phenotype | Normal or variable | Normal or variable | Often increased | Low or inappropriate | Iron studies are not defining. Look for other cytopenias, abnormal smear findings, persistent unexplained anemia, and marrow dysplasia or infiltration. [112] |
| Endocrine or systemic underproduction, including hypothyroidism | Normal or mildly macrocytic | Normal or mildly high | Variable | Low or inappropriately normal | Iron studies are usually normal unless a second process is present; thyroid, renal, inflammatory, medication, and nutritional testing directs the next step. [21] |

Hemolysis is the principal normocytic pattern with an increased reticulocyte response. A reticulocyte response can be blunted by iron deficiency, B12 or folate deficiency, renal disease, infection, or marrow failure, so an apparently normal reticulocyte percentage is not reassuring in a severely anemic patient. The corrected response or production index is more meaningful than the raw percentage. [34]

### Illustrative case study: autoimmune hemolytic anemia

In suspected autoimmune hemolytic anemia, BSH describes a coherent pattern: increased unconjugated bilirubin, increased reticulocytes, reduced haptoglobin, normal or increased LDH, and a film showing spherocytes, agglutination, or polychromasia. The DAT is usually positive for IgG, IgM, IgA, or complement, commonly C3d. [2]

The diagnostic decision is therefore to order the hemolysis panel and DAT together, not to classify the patient from MCV alone. BSH also notes DAT-negative AIHA caused by low-affinity antibody, low red-cell-bound antibody, or an immunoglobulin not tested, so a negative DAT does not end the evaluation when the biochemical and smear pattern remains compelling. The outcome is a mechanism-based classification with an explicit exception rather than a false exclusion.

### Illustrative case study: aplastic anemia

For aplastic anemia, the defining decision is not whether the MCV is normal or macrocytic. BSH identifies pancytopenia with hypocellular marrow as the hallmark and requires exclusion of alternate diagnoses such as hypoplastic MDS; severity is assessed with Camitta criteria. ASH's 2026 guideline similarly describes severe acquired aplastic anemia as a condition marked by pancytopenia and marrow hypocellularity. [48]; ASH 2026 aplastic anemia guideline

The decision is to escalate from a CBC pattern to marrow examination and a cytopenia workup when reticulocytes are profoundly inadequate or other cell lines are also low. Iron studies may be obtained to exclude a concurrent deficiency, but they do not establish aplasia. The outcome is separation of marrow failure from iron-restricted or hemolytic anemia.

**Decision-ready insight:** in normocytic anemia, the reticulocyte response and the other blood-cell lines usually provide more mechanistic information than small differences in MCV or MCH.

## Macrocytic and Megaloblastic Anemias

Macrocytosis reflects either megaloblastic ineffective erythropoiesis, nonmegaloblastic red-cell enlargement, or a high reticulocyte population. B12 and folate deficiency impair DNA synthesis and produce megaloblastic precursors; homocysteine and methylmalonic acid can help clarify the biochemical cause. [21]

| Cause | MCV | MCH | RDW | Reticulocytes | Iron studies and confirmatory clues |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vitamin B12 deficiency | High, sometimes with a mixed or normal mean if iron deficiency coexists | Often high because cells are large | Usually high | Low or inappropriately normal because ineffective erythropoiesis predominates | Iron studies may be normal; ferritin can be reduced if a concurrent iron deficiency exists. Check B12, folate, smear, and when needed methylmalonic acid and homocysteine. The BSH 2014 page is archived and was marked superseded by NICE in 2024. [43]; [21] |
| Folate deficiency | High or mixed | Often high | Usually high | Low or inappropriately normal | Iron studies are not primary; check folate and the megaloblastic film, and distinguish from B12 deficiency before treatment. [43]; [21] |
| Alcohol-related macrocytosis | High | Often high | Variable | Low or normal unless a second hemolytic or bleeding process exists | No characteristic iron-deficiency pattern; assess alcohol exposure, liver tests, nutrition, and other causes. [22] |
| Liver disease | High or variable | Often high | Variable | Low or normal | Iron indices are variable and may reflect liver disease or iron loading rather than deficiency; use liver tests and smear. [21] |
| Hypothyroidism | Normal or high | Normal or high | Variable | Low or inappropriately normal | Iron studies are not diagnostic; thyroid testing is required. [21] |
| Drug-related macrocytosis | High or variable | Often high | Variable | Low or inappropriately normal | Iron studies are usually not the primary discriminator; review drugs that impair DNA synthesis or marrow function. [22] |
| Myelodysplastic syndrome | High, normal, or variable | Normal or high | Often high | Low or inappropriate | Iron indices are variable and do not diagnose MDS. Persistent macrocytic anemia with other cytopenias or dysplastic smear findings requires marrow evaluation and exclusion of B12, folate, iron, renal, thyroid, drug, and inflammatory causes. [112] |
| Reticulocytosis from hemolysis or recent blood loss | Normal or high because reticulocytes are larger | Often high | High | Increased | Iron studies are not the primary discriminator; use the hemolysis panel or bleeding assessment. A reticulocyte count above 2% is associated with hyperproliferation in the retrieved macrocytosis reference, but the percentage must be corrected for the degree of anemia. [22]; [34] |
| Acquired sideroblastic anemia or MDS with ring sideroblasts | Often high, but may be variable | Variable | Often high | Low or inappropriate | Iron, ferritin, and TSAT are often increased; marrow ring sideroblasts and the underlying clonal or toxic cause establish the diagnosis. [83]; [81] |

A normal reticulocyte response is not expected in untreated megaloblastic anemia because the central problem is ineffective production. Conversely, reticulocytosis can raise MCV without a primary macrocytic marrow disorder. That distinction prevents a hemolytic episode from being mislabeled as B12 deficiency and prevents B12 deficiency from being dismissed because the MCV is only mildly high.

**Decision-ready insight:** macrocytosis should be split into megaloblastic, nonmegaloblastic, and reticulocyte-driven pathways. The blood film and reticulocyte response are the shortest route to the correct branch.

## Hemoglobinopathies and Other Inherited Hemolytic Anemias

Thalassemia belongs in the microcytic branch because globin-chain underproduction produces low MCV and MCH, but sickle syndromes are best classified with hemoglobin analysis and hemolysis findings. MCV and MCH cannot identify the sickle genotype. ASH educational material provides illustrative electrophoresis patterns that are more specific than the CBC alone. [74]; [26]

| ASH educational electrophoresis pattern | Hemoglobin fractions and CBC values reported in the retrieved ASH material | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| HbSS | HbS >90%, HbF <10%, HbA2 <3.5%, HbA 0%; hemoglobin 6-9 g/dL; MCV >80 fL | A sickle disease pattern with anemia and hemolysis; evaluate the reticulocyte response and hemolysis markers separately. |
| HbSC | HbS 50%, HbC 50%, HbA 0%; hemoglobin 10-15 g/dL; MCV 75-85 fL | The CBC is less severely anemic than the HbSS pattern in the retrieved table, but fractions establish the genotype. |
| HbS beta-plus thalassemia | HbS >60%, HbF <20%, HbA2 >3.5%, HbA 10-30%; hemoglobin 9-12 g/dL; MCV <75 fL | The low MCV points toward the beta-thalassemia component; HbA is present. |
| HbS beta-zero thalassemia | HbS >80%, HbF <20%, HbA2 >3.5%, HbA 0%; hemoglobin 6-9 g/dL; MCV <70 fL | The pattern resembles severe sickle disease in anemia and absence of HbA, but the elevated HbA2 and low MCV support the beta-zero thalassemia component. |
| Sickle cell trait | HbA:HbS is usually around 60:40 | Trait is identified by hemoglobin fractions, not by an anemia CBC pattern. |

The reported hemoglobin and MCV values are educational pattern examples, not universal diagnostic cutoffs for every patient. Transfusion, alpha-thalassemia, iron deficiency, treatment, age, and clinical state can alter a measured pattern; therefore, the hemoglobin fractions and clinical history must be interpreted together. [74]

### Illustrative case study: HbS beta-plus thalassemia

A patient with a sickle syndrome and microcytosis should not be assumed to have iron deficiency. In the ASH educational pattern, HbS beta-plus thalassemia has HbA present at 10-30%, HbA2 above 3.5%, and MCV below 75 fL, whereas HbSS has HbA at 0% and MCV above 80 fL in the reported pattern. [26]

The decision is to obtain or review hemoglobin analysis and then use ferritin and TSAT to evaluate a possible superimposed iron deficiency. The outcome is a distinction between a globin-production pattern and an acquired iron-restricted pattern; iron studies alone cannot determine the sickle genotype.

Other inherited hemolytic anemias, including red-cell membrane and enzyme disorders, often enter the normocytic hemolytic branch: reticulocytes are increased when marrow compensation is intact, RDW may rise with polychromasia, and the diagnostic tests are smear, hemolysis markers, enzyme or membrane testing, and family history. The BSH AIHA panel is useful for recognizing hemolysis, but a positive DAT specifically supports immune hemolysis and does not diagnose every inherited disorder. [2]

**Decision-ready insight:** a hemoglobinopathy can produce a distinctive MCV pattern, but hemoglobin analysis is the decisive test. Use iron studies to identify coexisting iron deficiency or iron loading, not to replace electrophoresis or molecular testing.

## Mixed Patterns, Exceptions, and Safety Checks

A practical table must be treated as a set of typical signatures. Several common situations break the apparent pattern:

1. **Mixed iron and B12 or folate deficiency:** microcytic and macrocytic populations can average to a normal MCV while RDW is high. In that setting, a normal MCV does not exclude either deficiency; inspect the smear and order both pathways.
2. **Inflammation plus absolute iron deficiency:** inflammation can raise ferritin, while true iron stores are depleted. WHO recommends accounting for inflammation when interpreting ferritin; in infection or inflammation, the retrieved WHO guidance uses higher deficiency thresholds of <30 ug/L in children and <70 ug/L in adults. [62]
3. **Reticulocytosis:** large reticulocytes can raise MCV and RDW. A macrocytic result with an increased reticulocyte response should prompt bleeding and hemolysis assessment before a megaloblastic label. [22]
4. **Recent transfusion:** donor and patient red cells create a mixed population and can alter MCV and RDW. The pretransfusion CBC, smear, reticulocytes, and iron history are more informative than a posttransfusion mean alone.
5. **Pregnancy, children, and older adults:** use the relevant WHO hemoglobin cutoff and age-specific laboratory reference ranges. Do not transplant the adult male or nonpregnant female thresholds into a child or pregnant patient. [16]
6. **Ferritin and iron overload:** high ferritin is not synonymous with iron overload, and inflammation can raise it. Conversely, sideroblastic disease, chronic transfusion, and some hemoglobinopathies can cause iron loading; interpret ferritin with TSAT, liver status, transfusion history, and the disease context. [62]; [81]
7. **Guideline currency:** the BSH site states that its 2014 B12 and folate guideline has been superseded by the NICE vitamin B12 guideline published in 2024. Use the BSH page as historical BSH context and current national guidance for active clinical decisions. [43]

The exact universal numerical reticulocyte cutoff is not a safe substitute for an absolute count or production index because the percentage changes with the number of circulating red cells and the severity of anemia. Similarly, a single ferritin threshold cannot perform equally in a healthy person and a person with infection, inflammation, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, or transfusion exposure.

**Decision-ready insight:** when the CBC pattern is internally inconsistent, assume a mixed process until the smear, reticulocyte response, iron context, and disease-specific tests explain the discrepancy.

## Synthesis

| Diagnostic layer | WHO contribution | BSH contribution | ASH contribution | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does the patient meet the anemia definition? | Population-specific hemoglobin cutoffs and severity guidance | Uses anemia definitions within disease-specific guidance | Uses disease-specific diagnostic context | Hemoglobin alone does not identify mechanism. |
| Is iron unavailable? | Ferritin interpretation and inflammation adjustments | Iron-deficiency and disease-specific laboratory practice, with the archived B12 page and current disease guidelines | Educational and disease-specific iron or hemoglobinopathy material | Ferritin is sensitive to inflammation, so low serum iron alone is nonspecific. |
| Is the marrow responding? | Not the main focus of the retrieved WHO documents | BSH AIHA and aplastic guidance use reticulocyte and marrow-response concepts | ASH disease-specific guidance covers aplasia and hemoglobinopathies | Reticulocytosis supports loss or destruction, but inadequate response can occur in combined disease. |
| What is the morphology? | WHO does not supply one etiologic MCV matrix | BSH disease guidance identifies characteristic findings such as AIHA and aplasia | ASH educational material gives hemoglobinopathy and sideroblastic patterns | MCV, MCH, and RDW are patterns, not diagnoses. |
| What confirms the cause? | Ferritin and Hb thresholds | DAT, marrow assessment, biochemical testing, and disease-specific investigations | Hemoglobin electrophoresis, molecular or marrow-oriented disease workups | Confirmatory tests are more specific but are more resource-intensive. |

Three tensions determine the safest interpretation. First, morphology is fast but nonspecific: iron deficiency, thalassemia, inflammation, lead toxicity, and sideroblastic anemia can all be microcytic. Second, reticulocytes reveal mechanism but not the cause: hemolysis, bleeding, and some hemoglobinopathies can all increase them. Third, ferritin is useful for iron stores but rises with inflammation, so the same ferritin value can mean different things in different clinical contexts. Evaluation of microcytosis - PubMed; [62]; [2]

The integrated recommendation is a four-stage workflow: **WHO hemoglobin gate -> CBC and smear morphology -> absolute reticulocyte response -> ferritin plus TSAT, TIBC or transferrin, serum iron, and disease-specific confirmation**. Add B12 and folate testing for macrocytosis or unexplained high RDW, hemolysis testing for an increased reticulocyte response, hemoglobin analysis for disproportionate microcytosis or sickle-pattern anemia, and marrow examination for persistent unexplained cytopenia or an inadequate reticulocyte response.

The evidence base is deliberately layered rather than falsely uniform. WHO provides the population definition of anemia and ferritin context; BSH provides focused clinical guidelines for AIHA and aplastic anemia and flags its B12/folate document as superseded; ASH provides the retrieved hemoglobinopathy and sideroblastic educational material plus a current aplastic-anemia guideline record. The table is therefore most reliable as a decision aid when each row is followed by the confirmatory test named in that row.
